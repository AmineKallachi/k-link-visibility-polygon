1Introduction

A sensor q inside a simple polygon P sees every point it has a straight line of sight to. Together these points form its visibility polygon. Now let the line of sight bend. A point is k-link visible from q if a polygonal path of at most k segments, called links, connects them inside P: a message relayed k − 1 times, or a robot allowed k − 1 turns.

Mahdavi, Mohades and Kouhestani (2011) add a twist: one edge e of P is a mirror. A line of sight that hits it is reflected, angle in equal to angle out, and the reflection does not count as a link. The question is which points of P the sensor reaches with at most k links in that setting.

Figure 1 shows the effect in a polygon shaped like a corridor with three bays. With one link, q sees its own bay and a strip of the corridor; with two, a relay in the corridor sees into the other bays. With a mirror on the corridor floor, a single link already reaches into the middle bay, and two links cover most of it.

The paper computes this region in linear time, independently of k. That is optimal, since reading the input takes as long. Its key observation is that the mirror, which seems to complicate every level, only matters for two of the k links.

This page follows their algorithm step by step and describes my implementation of it. Figures 2 to 4 are interactive and share one polygon, sensor and mirror: editing one updates all three.

2Seeing around corners

Without a mirror, k-link visibility grows level by level, and the shape of the visibility polygon drives the growth (Figure 2). Only reflex vertices, with an interior angle above 180°, can block the view. Where the line of sight grazes one, the visibility polygon ends in an edge that crosses the interior of P instead of following its boundary: a window.

Each window splits P in two. The part q cannot see is the window's pocket, and any path into a pocket has to cross its window. That is exactly what the second link needs. It starts at a relay inside the visibility polygon, so to reach a point in a pocket it must cross that pocket's window. The two-link region is therefore the visibility polygon plus, for every window, the part of its pocket the window sees: its weak visibility polygon, the points that see at least one point of the window.

A window thus acts as a sensor in its own right, a segment instead of a point. This is why the algorithms below take a general sensor O: a point, a segment, or a polygon with m vertices.

The third link repeats the argument with the windows of the two-link region, and so on, one ring of pockets per level. The smallest number of links that reaches a point is its link distance. Windows and pockets are the whole toolkit; the rest of the page is about what a mirror does to them.

3Adding a mirror

Now one edge e of P is a mirror. A line of sight that hits it is reflected and continues. It can be reflected only once, because after the reflection it moves away from the line through e.

The obvious approach is to reflect the polygon instead of the light. Glue a mirrored copy of P onto e, and a reflected line of sight becomes a straight segment into the copy. k-link visibility with a mirror then becomes ordinary k-link visibility in the doubled polygon, folded back afterwards.

This works when P lies entirely on one side of the line through e, but not in general. If part of P lies beyond that line, around a reflex vertex next to the mirror for instance, the copy overlaps P itself, and the doubled polygon is no longer simple, which the linear-time visibility algorithms require. Moreover, every relay found in the copy stands for a real point, from which the next link may stay on its side or cross over. Each new level then means merging four overlapping regions, and there is no known way to do that in linear time (Mahdavi et al. 2011).

The paper therefore uses the reflection only locally, at the mirror itself, starting with a single link.

4One link with a mirror

With one link, q sees what it sees directly plus what it sees in the mirror. For the second part, the classic trick from optics applies. A ray reflected at a point r of e continues exactly like the straight line from q′, the reflection of q in e, through r. So q sees in the mirror what q′ would see if e were a window into P.

Two restrictions make this exact. The reflection point must be visible from q, so q′ only looks through the part of e on the boundary of q's visibility polygon. And the edges of P between q′ and the mirror do not exist in the mirror world, so q′ looks through them. 

function mirrorVisibility(P, e, q):
    V  = visibilityPolygon(P, q)          // what q sees directly
    m  = part of e on the boundary of V   // the piece of mirror q sees
    if m is empty: return V
    q' = reflect(q, e)
    T  = what q' sees through m           // ignoring edges in front of e
    return union(V, T)

Kouhestani et al. (2010) make every line of this linear. For what q′ sees, they build a helper polygon, the part of P behind the mirror with q′ as an extra vertex, and run a linear-time visibility algorithm in it. Both regions meet the boundary of P in the same order, so one walk along the boundary computes their union (Lemma 1). One link with a mirror thus takes O(n) time for a polygon with n vertices (Theorem 2), also when the sensor is a segment (Theorem 3). For a polygonal sensor it takes O(n + m) (Mahdavi et al. 2011, Lemma 1). My implementation uses a rotational sweep instead, at the cost of a log n factor; Section 8 explains why.

5Two links

A point reached with two links is reached through one relay x, and each link is either direct or reflected. The paper sorts the resulting paths into four groups, writing the last link first, with −e for a direct link and +e for a reflected one:

  • {−e, −e}: both links direct, as in Section 2;
  • {−e, +e}: the first link reflected, the second direct;
  • {+e, −e}: the first link direct, the second reflected;
  • {+e, +e}: both links reflected.

Computing the groups separately is where the difficulty lies. As soon as the second link is reflected, every candidate relay has its own reflection, so there is no single q′ to look from. And the groups overlap heavily, so they would still have to be merged afterwards, the same problem that defeats unfolding.

The paper avoids the groups and returns to pockets. Consider the windows of the one-link region with the mirror. Every new point lies in the pocket of some window w, and its last link crosses w. If the pocket contains no part of the mirror, the link cannot be reflected after crossing w, so the point lies in the weak visibility polygon of w (Lemma 3). If the pocket does contain part of the mirror, the link may still be reflected there: the point is seen from w directly or via that part of e. That is the one-link problem of Section 4 inside the pocket, with w as the sensor (Lemma 4).

Since e is a single segment, it intersects at most two windows (Lemma 2), so at most two pockets involve the mirror at all. The one-link region together with what each window sees in its pocket is exactly the two-link region; the paper verifies this group by group (Lemma 5). 

function twoLinks(P, e, O):           // Algorithm 3
    V = mirrorVisibility(P, e, O)     // one link
    for each window w of V:
        pocket = part of P behind w
        m = part of e inside pocket   // empty for all but two windows
        V = union(V, mirrorVisibility(pocket, m, w))
    return V

Each pocket costs time linear in its size, and pockets share only vertices, so the total is O(n + m) (Theorem 6). One discrepancy: the printed Algorithm 3 iterates over the windows of the visibility polygon without the mirror, while the lemmas use the windows of the region with the mirror. I follow the lemmas; Section 8 explains why.

6Any number of links

Without a mirror, the level-by-level growth of Section 2 can be done for all levels at once. The window partition divides P into regions of equal link distance in O(n + m) time (Lemma 7; Suri 1986).

With a mirror, let ℓ be the link distance from O to e, the number of links needed to reach the mirror at all. The levels then fall into three phases:

  • below ℓ links the mirror is out of reach: ordinary link visibility;
  • at ℓ links the last link can be reflected: Section 4, with everything reached in ℓ − 1 links as the sensor;
  • at ℓ + 1 links the last two links can be reflected: Section 5, with the same sensor.

From level ℓ + 2 on, the mirror adds nothing. With ℓ links some point of e is reached, and one more link along e reaches all of it. After ℓ + 1 links every possible reflection point is therefore already reached, and a reflected link could just as well start at its reflection point and continue straight (Lemma 8). The remaining levels are ordinary link visibility inside the pockets. 

function kLinks(P, e, O, k):                   // Algorithm 4.2
    ℓ = linkDistance(O, e)
    if k < ℓ: return kLinksNoMirror(P, O, k)   // mirror out of reach
    Q = kLinksNoMirror(P, O, ℓ - 1)
    if k == ℓ: return mirrorVisibility(P, e, Q)
    R = twoLinks(P, e, Q)                      // links ℓ and ℓ + 1
    for each window w of R:                    // no mirror from here on
        R = union(R, kLinksNoMirror(pocket(w), w, k - ℓ - 1))
    return R

Every step is linear and the pockets share only vertices, so the whole algorithm runs in O(n + m) time for every k (Theorem 9). Figure 4 shows the result for the shared polygon; the line below it gives ℓ and the levels at which the mirror is used.

7Conclusion

A mirror seems to make link visibility much harder, but Mahdavi et al. show that it does not: reasoning about windows and pockets instead of kinds of paths confines the mirror to two levels, and the rest is ordinary link visibility. Implementing the algorithm revealed one discrepancy, the windows used in Algorithm 3 (Section 8).

The paper ends with the case of several mirrors, where a beam can be reflected back and forth between them. Without a bound on the number of reflections it is not even clear how to compute the visibility polygon of a point, and the k-link case with at most t reflections per beam is left as future work. Linear time cannot carry over in general: when every edge is a mirror, a single reflection already yields a region of complexity Θ(n²) (Aronov et al. 1998). As far as I could find, the problem is still open.

8Design decisions

The goal of this implementation is to understand and illustrate the paper's algorithms, not to reach their optimal running time. Their structure is kept: the link distance ℓ, the two levels where the mirror matters, and Algorithm 3 over the windows. Several steps, however, are deliberately not implemented at their ideal complexity. Where the paper calls a linear-time subroutine from earlier work, I used a simpler method that computes the same region, or a close approximation of it. The optimal versions would not add to the understanding of the paper, so I did not consider them necessary.

One link with a mirror

Kouhestani et al. build a helper polygon behind the mirror, with q′ as an extra vertex, and run a linear-time visibility algorithm in it. I compute the same region with one rotational sweep from q′ over the visible part of the mirror, in O(n log n) time. This avoids the helper polygon, whose vertices are not integers and which has q′ on its boundary, and keeps every orientation test exact on integer input. The union with the direct view uses the Clipper library instead of the linear walk along the boundary.

Two links (Algorithm 3)

The printed Algorithm 3 loops over the windows of the visibility polygon without the mirror, while Lemmas 3–5 use the windows of the region with the mirror. Where the direct and the reflected view meet, new windows appear that only exist with the mirror; the printed loop never looks behind them and misses points there that two links reach. My implementation follows the lemmas.

Pockets are not built explicitly. Each point on a window may use the whole mirror rather than only the part inside its pocket, which can only add points that two links reach, so the result is unchanged. Weak visibility from a window, linear-time in the paper (Guibas et al. 1987), is approximated by sampling points every 2 px along the window and taking the union of their visibility polygons. This is the one approximate step, and denser sampling cannot make it exact: next to a shadow edge through two reflex vertices, a point sees only a sliver of the window, which shrinks as the point approaches the edge. The error is one-sided: points near shadow edges may be missed, but no point is added that is not reached.

Any number of links (Algorithm 4.2)

The link distance ℓ, the two levels where the mirror is used, and ignoring the mirror from level ℓ + 2 on (Lemma 8) follow the paper exactly. Instead of the window partition, which computes all levels in linear time (Suri 1986), the levels without the mirror are grown one at a time through their windows, and ℓ is found the same way, by growing levels until one touches the mirror. The regions are the same; only the running time now depends on k.

Running time

Mine is O(k · s · n log n) plus the unions, where s is the number of sample points per level. Since s grows with the length of the windows in pixels, the same polygon scaled up takes longer: the bound depends on the coordinates, not only on n, which makes it pseudo-polynomial. For polygons with a few dozen vertices this takes a few tens of milliseconds. The general building blocks follow the same choice: a rotational sweep for visibility polygons (de Berg et al. 2008) instead of Lee's linear-time algorithm, and exact sign tests on integer input.

9Related work

The paper builds directly on Kouhestani et al. (2010), who compute the visibility polygon of a point or a segment with one mirror in linear time. Its other building blocks are classic linear-time results: visibility from a point (Lee 1983, corrected by Joe and Simpson 1987), weak visibility from a segment (Guibas et al. 1987), and minimum-link paths (Suri 1986), the basis of the window partition surveyed by Maheshwari et al. (2000). It also uses Bose et al. (2002) for the fact that a segment intersects at most two windows, and Langetepe et al. (2010) for visibility from a polygon.

For the implementation, my main source is de Berg et al. (2008), for the rotational sweep (also Asano 1985) and the basic predicates, with O'Rourke (1998) as a second reference. Bungiu et al. (2014) show that simple visibility algorithms often beat the linear-time ones in practice. The exact signs in the mirror sweep come from Shewchuk's robust predicates (1997), and the unions from Clipper, an implementation of Vatti's clipping algorithm (1992).

10Lemmas and theorems

The statements cited above, in the paper's notation. P is a simple polygon with n vertices, e the mirror, and O the sensor: a point, segment or polygon with m vertices. VP(O) is what O sees, VP,e(O) what it sees directly or in the mirror, and a superscript k, as in VkP,e(O), allows k links.

Kouhestani et al. (2010)

Lemma 1. The union of two visibility polygons inside P can be computed in O(n) time.

Theorem 2. VP,e(q) can be computed in O(n) time.

Theorem 3. The weak visibility polygon of a segment, with the mirror, can be computed in O(n) time.

Mahdavi et al. (2011)

Lemma 1. For a polygon Q with m vertices inside P, VP,e(Q) can be computed in O(n + m) time.

Lemma 2. At most two windows of VP,e(O) intersect e.

Lemma 3. If the pocket of a window w of VP,e(O) does not intersect e, every point of V2P,e(O) in that pocket is directly visible from w.

Lemma 4. If the pocket of a window w of VP,e(O) intersects e, the points of V2P,e(O) in that pocket are exactly Vpocket(w),e(w).

Lemma 5. Adding Vpocket(w),e∩pocket(w)(w) to VP,e(O) for every window w of VP,e(O) gives V2P,e(O).

Theorem 6. V2P,e(O) can be computed in O(n + m) time.

Lemma 7. VkP(O), without a mirror, can be computed in O(n + m) time.

Lemma 8. If ℓ is the link distance between O and e and Q = Vℓ+1P,e(O), then VP,e(Q) = VP(Q).

Theorem 9. VkP,e(O) can be computed in O(n + m) time, for every k.
