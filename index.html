k-Link Visibility with a Reflective Edge

An interactive companion to S. S. Mahdavi, A. Mohades and B. Kouhestani, Computing k-Link Visibility Polygons in Environments with a Reflective Edge (CCCG 2011)

Amine Kallachi
INFO-F420 Computational Geometry, Université libre de Bruxelles, 2026

1Introduction

Place a sensor q inside a room, modelled as a simple polygon P with n corners. A point p of the room is visible from q when the open segment qp lies in the interior of P; the paper uses this clear visibility, so a line of sight may not touch the boundary. All visible points together form the visibility polygon V(q), written VP(q) when the room matters: the part of the room that a camera at q films, or that a lamp at q lights. Computing it is a classic problem of computational geometry, and it can be done in linear time (Lee 1983).

k-link visibility lets the line of sight bend. A point p is k-link visible from q when a path of at most k straight segments, called links, leads from q to p without leaving the room. Each bend is an intermediate point that sees the previous one and the next one. For k = 1 this is ordinary visibility; for k = 2 the sensor may look around one corner. The region of all such points is the k-link visibility polygon Vk(q). One way to picture it is a message passed on by k − 1 relays, or a robot that may change direction k − 1 times. The smallest k that reaches a point is its link distance from q.

The paper adds one reflective edge e, a mirror. A line of sight that hits e bounces off with equal angles, and the bounce does not count as an extra link: two points that see each other through the mirror are 1-link visible. In Figure 1(c) the mirror lets q look into the middle room with a single link, and the second link reaches further still.

Mahdavi et al. compute the k-link visibility polygon with a mirror, VkP,e(q), in linear time, O(n + m), where the sensor may also be a segment or a polygon with m corners. The running time does not depend on k, and it is optimal. Gluing the room to its mirror image along e would turn the problem into ordinary k-link visibility, but only when the whole room lies on one side of the line through e. When the room crosses that line and the mirror ends in reflex corners, the glued room is no longer a simple polygon, and each new level would need the union of four polygons.

The key observation of the paper avoids this: when Vk is built level by level, the mirror only matters at two levels. All other levels are ordinary k-link visibility (Maheshwari et al. 2000), and the two special levels reuse the visibility polygon with a mirror of Kouhestani et al. (2010).

The sections below follow this construction step by step. Lemmas, theorems and algorithms keep their numbers from the paper. Figures 2 to 4 share one room and one sensor: draw a new room in any of them, or drag a corner or the sensor, and every figure follows. Parts that are not implemented yet are marked To do.

2Direct visibility

A window of V(q) starts where the view grazes a reflex corner and continues to a wall further away. The part of the room behind a window is a pocket: the sensor cannot look into it directly. Pockets are where the next link has to go.

To compute V(q), a ray turns around q and stops at every corner of the room, in order of angle. A balanced search tree keeps the walls that the ray crosses, closest first. Each time the closest wall changes, the boundary of V(q) jumps along a window. For a room with n corners this rotational sweep takes O(n log n) time (de Berg et al. 2008, §15.2). All tests use exact arithmetic on whole-number coordinates, so a corner that lies exactly on a line of sight is still handled correctly.

To check the result independently, tick Check with brute force. Every point of a fine grid is then tested on its own: a ray is cast from q towards the point, and the point is visible if the ray reaches it before a wall stops it. Visible points are green and hidden points grey. When the sweep is correct, the green points fill exactly the yellow region.

3Seeing through a mirror To do

One wall e is now a mirror. Two points x and y see each other through e when some point r of e sees both, and the segments xr and yr lie on opposite sides of the inward normal of e at r and make the same angle with it. The point r is not an intermediate point, so x and y are still 1-link visible. What the sensor sees in the mirror is therefore exactly what its mirror image q′ sees through the part of e that q itself sees.

The region VP,e(q) is the union of VP(q) and the part of the room seen through the mirror. Kouhestani et al. (2010) compute it in O(n) time for a point or a segment, and merge the two parts in linear time by walking along both boundaries in order. For a polygon O, Mahdavi et al. first compute VP(O) with the algorithm of Langetepe et al. (2010) and then add the parts that O sees through e.

Lemma 1. Let Q be a simple polygon with m corners inside P. Then VP,e(Q) can be computed in O(n + m) time.

The windows of VP,e(q) behave a little differently from those of V(q). A window may now bend at an interior point (Mahdavi et al. 2011, Figure 2), and only few windows can reach the mirror. Any segment inside P crosses at most two windows of a visibility polygon (Bose et al. 2002), and the mirror is such a segment:

Lemma 2. At most two windows of VP,e(O) intersect the mirror e.

4k-link visibility with a mirror To do

The paper first builds k-link visibility without the mirror, then handles two links with the mirror, and finally combines both for any k.

4.1Without the mirror

V1(q) is the visibility polygon of Figure 2, and V2(q) adds everything visible from some point of it. Such a new point lies in a pocket of V(q) and is seen from the window of that pocket. Each level therefore needs the weak visibility polygon WVP(s) of a segment s: the points of P that see some point of s other than its endpoints. Guibas et al. (1987) compute it in linear time.

Repeating this level by level gives the window partitioning: P is cut into regions whose points have the same link distance from q, in O(n) time (Maheshwari et al. 2000). The same works for an object O. The link distance between O and a segment s is 1 when no window of VP(O) has s completely inside its pocket, and otherwise 1 plus the link distance between s and that window. The link distance between two segments takes O(n) time (Suri 1986; Maheshwari et al. 2000).

Lemma 7. Let O be an object of complexity m inside P. Then VkP(O) can be computed in O(n + m) time.

4.2Two links with the mirror

A point of V2P,e(O) is reached through one intermediate point, and each of the two links can be direct (−e) or go through the mirror (+e). This gives four groups of points: {−e, −e}, {+e, −e}, {−e, +e} and {+e, +e}. As before, every new point is seen from a window of VP,e(O), and the next lemmas say what each window adds to its pocket.

Lemma 3. If the pocket of a window wi of VP,e(O) does not meet e, every point of V2P,e(O) in that pocket is seen directly from wi. Other windows add nothing there, and neither does the mirror.

Lemma 4. If the pocket of wi meets e, the points of V2P,e(O) in that pocket are exactly Vpocket(wi),e(wi), the region that wi sees inside its pocket, with the mirror.

Lemma 5. Adding Vpocket(wi),ei(wi) to VP,e(O) for every window wi, where ei is the part of e inside its pocket, gives exactly V2P,e(O). The proof checks each of the four groups.

Algorithm 3. V2P,e(O)

  1. Compute VP,e(O).
  2. Let w1, …, wd be the windows of VP(O), and ei the part of e inside pocket(wi).
  3. Compute every Vpocket(wi),ei(wi) and add it to VP,e(O).
  4. Return the result.

Step 1 takes O(n + m) time by Lemma 1. In step 3 a pocket with ni corners takes O(ni) time, and two pockets share at most one corner, so the ni add up to O(n).

Theorem 6. V2P,e(O) can be computed in O(n + m) time, where m is the complexity of O.

4.3Any number of links

Let ℓ be the link distance between O and the mirror e. The paper calls it m, but here m is already the size of O. Below level ℓ no path reaches the mirror, so those levels are ordinary k-link visibility. At levels ℓ and ℓ + 1 the mirror is reachable, and Section 3 and Algorithm 3 apply. After that, the pockets of the region no longer meet e, so the mirror adds nothing more:

Lemma 8. Let Q = Vℓ+1P,e(O). Then VP,e(Q) = VP(Q).

Algorithm 4.2. VkP,e(O)

  1. Compute the link distance ℓ between O and e.
  2. If k < ℓ, return VkP(O).
  3. Otherwise:
    1. Compute Q = Vℓ−1P(O).
    2. If k = ℓ, return VP,e(Q).
    3. Compute R = V2P,e(Q).
    4. Let w1, …, wd be the windows of R.
    5. Compute every Vk−ℓ−1pocket(wi)(wi) and add it to R.
    6. Return the result.

R is Vℓ+1P,e(O), so by Lemma 8 step 3(e) can ignore the mirror. Steps 3(a) to 3(d) take O(n + m) time by Lemma 7, Lemma 1 and Theorem 6. In step 3(e) a pocket with ni corners takes O(ni) time, and the ni add up to at most n.

Theorem 9. VkP,e(O) can be computed in O(n + m) time, for every k from 1 to n. The running time does not depend on k.

The paper ends with open questions. With two or more mirrors a beam can bounce back and forth, and it is not clear how to compute the visibility polygon of a point when the number of reflections is unlimited. The authors name as future work the k-link visibility polygon in a room with several mirrors, when each beam may reflect at most t times.
