1Introduction

Imagine a sensor, such as a camera, standing inside a room. It sees every point it can reach with a straight line of sight. Walls block its view, so some parts of the room stay hidden.

Now suppose the sensor can pass on what it sees. A relay picks up the signal and looks further, possibly around a corner. A point is k-link visible from the sensor if a path of at most k straight segments, called links, connects them inside the room. With k = 1 this is ordinary visibility. With k = 2 there is one relay in between, and so on. You can also picture a robot that may turn at most k − 1 times.

The paper by Mahdavi, Mohades and Kouhestani (2011) adds one more element: one wall of the room is a mirror. When a line of sight hits the mirror, it bounces off, just like light. The bounce is free: it does not count as an extra link. The question is which points the sensor can reach with at most k links when this mirror is present.

In Figure 1, the room is a corridor with three bays. With one link, q only sees its own bay and a strip of the corridor. With two links, a relay in the corridor can look into the other bays. With a mirror on the corridor floor, a single link already reaches into the middle bay.

The paper shows how to compute this region in linear time, whatever the value of k. This is optimal, since just reading the input takes linear time. The main insight is surprising: the mirror only matters for two of the k links.

This page explains their algorithm step by step and describes how I implemented it. Figures 3 to 5 are interactive. They share the same room, sensor and mirror, so a change in one figure shows up in all of them.

2Key terms

A few terms come back throughout the page. The room is a simple polygon P with n vertices, and q is the sensor.

Visibility polygon
All points of P that q sees directly. It is the yellow region in Figure 2(a).
Reflex vertex
A vertex with an interior angle larger than 180°. These corners stick into the room, and they are the only ones that can block the view.
Window
An edge of the visibility polygon that is not part of a wall. It starts at a reflex vertex that the line of sight just grazes, and crosses the open room.
Pocket
The part of the room behind a window. The sensor cannot see it, and the only way in is through the window.
Link distance
The smallest number of links needed to reach a point from the sensor.
Weak visibility polygon
The region seen by a segment, such as a window: every point that sees at least one point of the segment.

The sensor does not have to be a point. It can also be a segment or a polygon with m vertices. In that general case we call the sensor O.

3Link visibility without a mirror

Before adding the mirror, it helps to see how k-link visibility grows without one. The windows are the key.

A path with two links bends once. We call that bend the relay point: the first link goes from the sensor to the relay point, and the second link continues from there (Figure 2b). The relay point has to be somewhere the sensor sees, so it lies in the visibility polygon.

Now take a point in a pocket. To reach it, the second link has to enter the pocket, and the only way in is through the window. So everything the second link adds is seen through a window: the two-link region is the visibility polygon plus the weak visibility polygon of each window, inside its pocket. Figure 1(b) shows the result.

The third link works the same way, through the windows of the two-link region. Each new link adds one more ring of pockets. This is also why a window acts as a sensor of its own, and why the algorithms below accept a segment as the sensor.

4One link with a mirror

Now one edge e of the room is a mirror. A line of sight that hits it bounces off. It can bounce only once, because after the bounce it moves away from the mirror.

A natural first idea is to reflect the room instead of the light. Glue a mirrored copy of the room onto the mirror, and a bouncing line of sight becomes a straight line into the copy. Ordinary k-link visibility in the doubled room would then solve the problem.

Unfortunately, this only works if the whole room lies on one side of the mirror's line. If part of the room sticks out beyond it, the copy overlaps the room itself. The doubled shape is then no longer a simple polygon, which the fast visibility algorithms need. With more links there is a second problem. A relay point found in the copy stands for a real point in the room, and the next link from there can stay on its side or switch. Every new link then means merging four overlapping regions, and there is no known way to do that in linear time (Mahdavi et al. 2011).

So the reflection trick is only used locally, right at the mirror. With one link, q sees what it sees directly, the visibility polygon, plus what it sees in the mirror. Reflect q in the mirror to get a point q′. A ray from q that bounces at a point r of the mirror continues exactly like the straight line from q′ through r. So q sees in the mirror what q′ would see if the mirror were an open window into the room.

Two details matter. The bounce point must be visible from q, so q′ only looks through the part of the mirror that q sees. And the walls between q′ and the mirror do not exist in the mirror world, so they are ignored. 

function mirrorVisibility(P, e, q):
    V  = visibilityPolygon(P, q)          // what q sees directly
    m  = part of e on the boundary of V   // the piece of mirror q sees
    if m is empty: return V
    q' = reflect(q, e)
    T  = what q' sees through m           // ignoring edges in front of e
    return union(V, T)

Kouhestani et al. (2010) do every step in linear time. To find what q′ sees, they build a helper polygon: the part of the room behind the mirror, with q′ as an extra vertex. A linear-time visibility algorithm then runs inside it, and the two regions are merged by a single walk along the boundary of the room (Lemma 1).

In total, one link with a mirror takes O(n) time (Theorem 2), also when the sensor is a segment (Theorem 3). A polygonal sensor with m vertices takes O(n + m) time (Mahdavi et al. 2011, Lemma 1). My implementation uses a rotational sweep instead, which costs an extra log n factor; Section 8 explains why.

5Two links

A point reached with two links goes through one relay point x. Each of the two links can be direct or can bounce off the mirror. That gives four kinds of paths. Following the paper, we write −e for a direct link and +e for a link that bounces, with the last link first:

  • {−e, −e}: both links are direct, as in Section 3;
  • {−e, +e}: the first link bounces, the second is direct;
  • {+e, −e}: the first link is direct, the second bounces;
  • {+e, +e}: both links bounce.

Computing these four groups one by one is hard. When the second link bounces, every possible relay point has its own reflection, so there is no single q′ to work with. The groups also overlap a lot, so they would still need to be merged afterwards.

We can avoid the groups altogether by going back to pockets. Start from the one-link region with the mirror and look at its windows. Every new point lies in the pocket of some window w, and its last link has to cross w. There are two cases:

  • The pocket contains no part of the mirror. Then the link cannot bounce after crossing w, so the point is simply seen from w (Lemma 3).
  • The pocket contains part of the mirror. Then the link can still bounce there, so the point is seen from w either directly or through that piece of mirror. This is the one-link problem of Section 4 again, inside the pocket and with w as the sensor (Lemma 4).

The mirror is a single segment, so it crosses at most two windows (Lemma 2). This means that at most two pockets need the mirror at all. Putting all pockets together gives exactly the two-link region. Lemma 5 checks this for each of the four groups. 

function twoLinks(P, e, O):                          // Algorithm 3
    V = mirrorVisibility(P, e, O)                     // one link
    for each window w of V:
        pocket = part of P behind w
        m = part of e inside pocket                   // empty for all but two windows
        V = union(V, mirrorVisibility(pocket, m, w))
    return V

Each pocket takes time linear in its size, and the pockets only share vertices. So the total time is O(n + m) (Theorem 6).

One detail does not match. The printed Algorithm 3 loops over the windows of the visibility polygon without the mirror, while the lemmas use the windows of the region with the mirror. I follow the lemmas; Section 8 explains why.

6Any number of links

Without a mirror, the ring-by-ring growth of Section 3 can be done for all levels at once. The window partition splits the room into regions of equal link distance, in O(n + m) time (Lemma 7; Suri 1986).

With a mirror, let ℓ be the link distance from the sensor to the mirror: the number of links needed to reach it at all. The levels then fall into three phases:

  • Below ℓ links, the mirror cannot be reached. This is ordinary link visibility.
  • At ℓ links, the last link can bounce. This is the one-link case of Section 4, starting from everything reached with ℓ − 1 links.
  • At ℓ + 1 links, the last two links can bounce. This is the two-link case of Section 5, from the same start.

After that, the mirror adds nothing new. With ℓ links, some point of the mirror is reached. One more link along the mirror reaches all of it. So after ℓ + 1 links, every possible bounce point has already been reached. A link that bounces could just as well start at its bounce point and go straight (Lemma 8). From then on, it is ordinary link visibility again, inside the pockets. 

function kLinks(P, e, O, k):                   // Algorithm 4.2
    ℓ = linkDistance(O, e)
    if k < ℓ: return kLinksNoMirror(P, O, k)   // mirror out of reach
    Q = kLinksNoMirror(P, O, ℓ - 1)
    if k == ℓ: return mirrorVisibility(P, e, Q)
    R = twoLinks(P, e, Q)                      // links ℓ and ℓ + 1
    for each window w of R:                    // no mirror from here on
        R = union(R, kLinksNoMirror(pocket(w), w, k - ℓ - 1))
    return R

Every step takes linear time, and the pockets only share vertices. So the whole algorithm runs in O(n + m) time, for any k (Theorem 9). Figure 5 shows the result for the shared room. The line below the figure gives ℓ and the levels where the mirror is used.

7Conclusion

At first sight, a mirror seems to make link visibility much harder. Mahdavi et al. show that it does not. By reasoning about windows and pockets instead of kinds of paths, they limit the effect of the mirror to two levels. Everything else is ordinary link visibility. Implementing the algorithm revealed one mismatch, in the windows used by Algorithm 3 (Section 8).

The paper ends with the case of several mirrors. A beam can then bounce back and forth between them. Without a limit on the number of bounces, it is not even clear how to compute the visibility polygon of a single point. The k-link case with at most t bounces per beam is left as future work. Linear time cannot carry over in general: if every edge is a mirror, a single bounce can already give a region of complexity Θ(n²) (Aronov et al. 1998). As far as I could find, the problem is still open.

8Design decisions

The goal of this implementation is to understand and illustrate the paper's algorithms, not to reach their optimal running time. I kept their structure: the link distance ℓ, the two levels where the mirror matters, and Algorithm 3 over the windows. Some steps are deliberately not implemented at their ideal complexity. Where the paper relies on a linear-time subroutine from earlier work, I used a simpler method that computes the same region, or a close approximation of it. The optimal versions would not help to understand the paper, so I did not consider them necessary.

One link with a mirror

Kouhestani et al. build a helper polygon behind the mirror and run a linear-time visibility algorithm in it. I compute the same region with a single rotational sweep from q′, in O(n log n) time. This avoids the helper polygon, whose vertices are not integers. It also keeps every orientation test exact, because the input has integer coordinates. The direct and the mirrored region are merged with the Clipper library instead of a walk along the boundary.

Two links (Algorithm 3)

The printed Algorithm 3 loops over the windows of the visibility polygon without the mirror. Lemmas 3–5, however, use the windows of the region with the mirror. Where the direct view and the mirrored view meet, new windows appear that only exist with the mirror. The printed loop never looks behind them, so it misses points that two links do reach. My implementation follows the lemmas.

I do not build the pockets explicitly. Instead, every point on a window may use the whole mirror, not only the part inside its pocket. This can only add points that two links really reach, so the result stays the same.

The paper computes weak visibility from a window exactly, in linear time (Guibas et al. 1987). I approximate it: I take points every 2 px along the window and merge their visibility polygons. This is the only approximate step, and more samples cannot make it exact. Near a shadow edge through two reflex vertices, a point sees only a thin sliver of the window, and the sliver gets thinner the closer the point is to the edge. The error only goes one way: points close to a shadow edge may be missed, but no point is ever added by mistake.

Any number of links (Algorithm 4.2)

The link distance ℓ, the two levels with the mirror, and ignoring the mirror after level ℓ + 1 (Lemma 8) follow the paper exactly. The difference is in the levels without the mirror. The paper computes them all at once with the window partition (Suri 1986). I grow them one level at a time, through the windows of the previous level. I find ℓ the same way, by growing levels until one of them touches the mirror. The regions are the same; only the running time now depends on k.

Running time

My implementation runs in O(k · s · n log n) time, plus the cost of the unions. Here s is the number of sample points per level. Because s grows with the length of the windows in pixels, the same room drawn larger takes longer. The running time therefore depends on the coordinates and not only on n, which is called pseudo-polynomial. For rooms with a few dozen vertices, it takes a few tens of milliseconds.

The general building blocks follow the same choice: a rotational sweep for visibility polygons (de Berg et al. 2008) instead of Lee's linear-time algorithm, and exact sign tests on integer input.

9Related work

The paper builds directly on Kouhestani et al. (2010), who compute what a point or a segment sees with one mirror, in linear time. Its other building blocks are classic linear-time results. Lee (1983) computes the visibility polygon of a point, and Joe and Simpson (1987) corrected his algorithm. Guibas et al. (1987) compute weak visibility from a segment. Suri (1986) computes minimum-link paths, which lead to the window partition surveyed by Maheshwari et al. (2000). The paper also uses Bose et al. (2002) for the fact that a segment crosses at most two windows, and Langetepe et al. (2010) for visibility from a polygon.

For the implementation, my main source is de Berg et al. (2008). It gives the rotational sweep (also Asano 1985) and the basic geometric tests, with O'Rourke (1998) as a second reference. Bungiu et al. (2014) show that simple visibility algorithms often beat the linear-time ones in practice. The exact signs in the mirror sweep use Shewchuk's robust predicates (1997), and the unions use Clipper, an implementation of Vatti's clipping algorithm (1992).

10Lemmas and theorems

The statements cited above, in the paper's notation. P is a simple polygon with n vertices, e the mirror, and O the sensor: a point, segment or polygon with m vertices. VP(O) is what O sees, VP,e(O) what it sees directly or in the mirror, and a superscript k, as in VkP,e(O), allows k links.

Kouhestani et al. (2010)

Lemma 1. The union of two visibility polygons inside P can be computed in O(n) time.

Theorem 2. VP,e(q) can be computed in O(n) time.

Theorem 3. The weak visibility polygon of a segment, with the mirror, can be computed in O(n) time.

Mahdavi et al. (2011)

Lemma 1. For a polygon Q with m vertices inside P, VP,e(Q) can be computed in O(n + m) time.

Lemma 2. At most two windows of VP,e(O) intersect e.

Lemma 3. If the pocket of a window w of VP,e(O) does not intersect e, every point of V2P,e(O) in that pocket is directly visible from w.

Lemma 4. If the pocket of a window w of VP,e(O) intersects e, the points of V2P,e(O) in that pocket are exactly Vpocket(w),e(w).

Lemma 5. Adding Vpocket(w),e∩pocket(w)(w) to VP,e(O) for every window w of VP,e(O) gives V2P,e(O).

Theorem 6. V2P,e(O) can be computed in O(n + m) time.

Lemma 7. VkP(O), without a mirror, can be computed in O(n + m) time.

Lemma 8. If ℓ is the link distance between O and e and Q = Vℓ+1P,e(O), then VP,e(Q) = VP(Q).

Theorem 9. VkP,e(O) can be computed in O(n + m) time, for every k.
